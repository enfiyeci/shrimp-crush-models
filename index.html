Shrimp Crush Models

We built four computer models of shrimp being squashed in a trawl net. This page shows, step by step, what each one does, what it pretends is true, where each number came from, and what it can't do. It carries the results of the 27 October fixes.

Shared in November 2026 as part of the Sentient Futures Project Incubator. This is our working page of 28 October 2026, written for our mentor, Jan Gaida, so it answers his questions and a few sections were still in progress that day. Every percentage is a model estimate, not a measurement. Pictures of real boats are YouTube thumbnails: click one to watch the original video at the moment described.

The eight steps of a haul, each linked to a fishing film at that step. The models cover steps 1 to 5 (bright), roughly. Step 6, the bin, has only a rough stand-in: a dry pile on deck in model 1. None of them reaches sorting or the killing step (7 and 8), which is where Jan's question sits.

What the model shows

Big stern-ramp factory trawlers (Greenland, Norway) crush the most. Most of it happens after the bag leaves the water, when the catch suddenly weighs 20 to 39 times more (worked out from tissue densities). Greenland comes out most crushed in all 192 combinations of settings. These are model estimates. We weighted every combination by how well it fits five field studies of small hauls; the studies mostly trim the highest results but cannot pin the number down.

What we don't know

The actual share crushed. For Norway the model gives 47% at its default settings. Our best estimate, which counts every combination by how well it fits five field studies, is also 47%, and 90% of the fit weight lies between 27% and 75%. A stricter reading of one study alone gives 11% to 63%. It depends on things no one has measured for shrimp, like how a shell breaks and how a full bag lies on deck.

What would help most

Measurements that need no harm to any animal: published shell-strength data, photos or video of the bag on the ramp and the hoist with a scale in view, and counts of live and dead shrimp at the killing step.

Before you start

Words used here

Codend
The closed bag at the very end of a trawl net. The catch piles up in it.
Stern ramp
A slope at the back of big trawlers. The full bag is pulled up it onto the deck.
Lift
Lifting the bag into the air with a crane, over a hatch or a bin.
Crushed
The shell cracked under a broad squeeze, or was pierced by a neighbour's spine. Crushed does not mean dead.
kPa
A unit of pressure. 1 kPa is about the push of 10 cm of water standing on you.
N (newton)
A unit of force. 1 N is about the weight of a 100 g apple in your hand.
Pretend shrimp
A shrimp that exists only inside the computer.
Simulated haul (dice roll, draw)
One run of the model through a whole haul, where every unsure number is picked at random from its range.
Setting, combination
One way of making the seven choices nobody has measured, for example which force rule to use. There are 192 combinations.
Default settings
Each of those choices at its preferred value. The headline numbers use them.
Per animal
A big haul holds more shrimp, so it counts for more. Since 27 October the headline counts this way.
Shell strength
How hard you have to squeeze a shrimp before its shell cracks.
Luck factor
In a pile, a few shrimp carry far more than their share of the load. The model gives each shrimp a random factor for this.
Solid fraction
How much of a pile is actually shrimp. The rest is gaps full of water or air.
Rostrum
The pointed spike on a shrimp's head.
Fit weight
How well one combination matches five field studies of small hauls: 1 if its average sits inside every field figure, falling towards 0 the further outside it lands. Our best estimate counts each combination by this weight.
Strict Salini reading
Dropping every combination whose average crushes more than one field study (Salini et al. 2000) measured, plus two standard errors. 62 of 192 pass. We show it beside the best estimate as a check.
Alive at kill
The share of all kept shrimp reaching the killing step (cooker, freezer, ice or stunner) that still show any sign of life.
LAMMPS
A free physics program for simulating lots of small balls bumping into each other. Model 2 runs in it.
Codex
OpenAI's coding assistant. It wrote much of the model code and ran the five-stage test.

A mismatch the films show. On big factory trawlers the catch goes through a hatch into a tank of seawater below deck (Royal Greenland, Akamalik). The column model's last stage is a dry pile on deck. In a seawater tank the shrimp would float again, so the deck stage may overstate the load on those boats. The new tank option can test this once tank sizes are known.

Why the "Not covered" steps were left out

  • The models were built to calculate crushing: the load on a shell against its strength. The harms measured after the deck (air, heat, low oxygen, handling and time; see Alive at the killing step) are other kinds of harm.
  • There are no load or crushing numbers for those steps. None of the studies on this page measures loads or crushing in the bin, at sorting or at the killing step, and none counts all kept shrimp, alive and dead, just before the cooker, freezer or stunner. For emptying the bag, no source or film gives a drop height.
  • Models 2 and 3 had narrower jobs. Model 2 was built to measure how a squeezed pile shares its load. Model 3 takes pretend prawns only as far as a drop onto the deck, and it is paused.

Will they be added later?

  • No model of emptying the bag, sorting, grading or the killing step is planned yet. None of the plans on this page includes one.
  • What exists instead: the measured evidence and our judged share alive at kill, outside the models (Alive at the killing step).
  • Still open: feeding the model's oxygen result into that judgement, and testing the seawater-tank option, which waits for real tank sizes (both under Making model 1 better).

Model 1

The column model

This is the only model whose results we use. It ranks fisheries reliably, but it can't pin down the percentage crushed. Version of 27 October.

How it thinks, in five boxes

How heavy is the catch?

Weigh the catch in the bag, stage by stage. Under water it almost floats; out of water it is heavy.

Pressure on each slice

The slices at the bottom carry the weight of all the slices above, like the bottom book in a stack.

Force on one shrimp

Turn the slice's pressure into a push on one shrimp, with a luck factor for unlucky ones.

Strong enough?

Compare that push with how hard you need to squeeze this shrimp's shell to break it.

Count

If the push wins, the shrimp is crushed. Count the share, roll the dice again, 10,000 times.

How many pretend shrimp, and how many runs?

No pretend shrimp, by default. The column model does not follow single shrimp through the haul. It cuts the catch into 50 slices and works out, for each slice, what share of the shrimp in it would break. Only when shell weakening is switched on does it follow pretend shrimp: 128 per slice and shell type in every simulated haul.

What it repeats is the simulated haul. Each fishery's headline number is an average over 10,000 simulated hauls. We don't know exact values for things like catch size or shell strength, so each simulated haul picks them at random from their ranges (for Norway, between about 900 and 3,800 kg of catch per bag; step 0 below). Averaging many hauls makes the result steady from one run to the next. It does not make the model more correct: that depends on the assumptions explained below.

  • Headline numbers, default settings: 10,000 simulated hauls for each of the 7 fisheries, so 70,000. Each fishery's headline share is the average over its 10,000 hauls, with big hauls counting for more. For Norway the logbook catch sizes are already drawn in proportion to catch, so the model does not weight them by catch a second time.
  • The 27 October memo's 192 combinations: 1,000 simulated hauls for each combination and fishery, so 1.34 million. The memo's ranges come from these.
  • Our rerun of the 192 combinations: 500 simulated hauls for each combination and fishery, so 672,000. The dots and the best estimate on this page come from these.
  • In total: about 2.1 million simulated hauls behind the main results, before counting the one-at-a-time runs under What moves the numbers (1,000 hauls each) and the field-check runs (500 each). The runs at real catch sizes add about 11.9 million more (172 combinations, 69 catch sizes, 1,000 hauls each).

Step by step, with what it pretends

Steps 0 and A happen once. Steps 1 to 5 follow the bag through the haul. Steps 6 to 9 are the crush test, done at every stage.

0Roll the dice

We don't know most numbers exactly. So the model picks every unsure number at random from its range, runs once, and does that 10,000 times.

What a range is: each unsure number has three values in the model's parameter sheet: a low end, a best guess and a high end. In every dice roll the model picks a fresh value for each. For most numbers the pick always lies between the low and the high end, and how the picks spread depends on the number. For many, most picks land near the best guess and few near the ends (a hump). For some, every value in the range is equally likely (flat). The catch per bag is the exception: its low and high ends are the 5th and 95th percentiles, so about one roll in ten lands outside them. The chart under this step draws the main ranges.

What the model works out: a crushed share for each dice roll. The answer it reports is the spread of those results.

Changed on 27 October: many numbers are now picked mostly near their best guess (a hump-shaped curve), not evenly across the whole range. Before, a northern prawn averaged 4.95 g instead of its 4.36 g best guess, and large catches were picked too often.

It pretends that…

  • every number is picked on its own, as if none were connected. One exception since 27 October: a heavier shrimp now also gets a larger body.
  • "this haul was unusually big" and "we don't know the true value" are mixed into the same roll.

How the weight is built, and the guesses it rests on

  1. Every combination starts equally likely. Guess: we have no basis to prefer one before the field checks.
  2. Each check measures how far a combination's average lies outside the field figure, in units of the model's own spread for that check (its 5th to 95th percentile width ÷ 3.29). Inside the figure the distance is zero. Guess: the model's spread is the right yardstick for how badly a miss counts.
  3. Each study counts once. Its penalty is the average squared distance over its checks, so Larssen's six checks do not outvote the others. Guess: the five studies deserve equal say.
  4. Weight = e−½ × the sum of the five penalties. A combination that misses one study by one spread keeps 61% of its weight. Guess: a normal-shaped penalty.
  5. The field figures are the field-check team's reading of each study (table under Field checks). Leaving Fox out or using the original scoring's figures moves no best estimate by more than 4 points. Leaving out Noell, the hardest check, moves the most: Norway rises from 47% to 53%.

In plain words

  • Air plus heat harms fast; air in the cold much less. At 23 to 30 °C most Gulf shrimp no longer appeared normal after 15 minutes in air (impaired, not counted as dead), and dry-sorted seabob went from about half alive to almost none in 16 minutes. In cold air, northern prawn kept 80 to 90% survival after 15 minutes.
  • Warm water adds to the deaths of northern prawn. In an August catch, half were dead about 15 minutes after the haul, after a 2.5-hour tow; the authors also blame moulting, so warmth is not the only cause.
  • For cold-water catches, tow length matters as much as deck time. Short tows killed none; after 10 h tows about a third were dead, but they were first counted 3 h after hauling, so that figure includes those 3 hours.
  • Moulting shrimp die first.

What this suggests for stunners (our judgement)

  • Warm decks: after 15 minutes of dry sorting a stunner would probably reach few seabob still alive (almost none were alive after 16 minutes), and Gulf shrimp that are alive but impaired (most no longer appeared normal after 15 minutes in warm air; that study scored whether they appeared normal, not whether they were dead). One placed early, or faster and wetter sorting, would matter more.
  • Cold water, cooked soon after sorting: most kept shrimp are probably still alive at the cooker.
  • Large factory-trawler catches: crushing matters most here, but its effect on how many are alive at kill is unmeasured.

Model 2

The particle model

A physics simulation of single pretend prawns, run in LAMMPS. It was meant to measure how load is shared in a pile, for model 1. It is well built, but its numbers so far come from its settings, not from shrimp. No number on this page comes from it.

How many pretend prawns? 200 per run, and 500 in eight runs: 95 runs finished on 18 and 19 October across 8 species, about 21,400 prawns in all. Since 26 October the repair runs build piles of 1,000.

1Build one pretend prawn

Each pretend prawn is a rigid chain of 62 small balls, from 0.3 mm in the spike to 13 mm in the body, glued together in the shape of a northern prawn.

82 mm · 4.36 gBody length without the spike, and weight. The spike adds 27 mm.

It pretends that…

  • the body is rigid. Since 26 October the spike can bend and break; the body still can't.
  • one drawing of one male prawn gives the body depth for the whole species.
  • the other seven species are the northern prawn stretched or shrunk.
  • the body is as stiff as hard plastic, 300 MPa. Real shrimp are 10 to 100 times softer.

Model 3

The five-stage test

Codex took 100 pretend prawns from model 2 and pushed them through a whole haul in a small box, on 19 and 20 October. Every version failed at least one of its own checks, so it tells us nothing about crushing. It is paused.

What it pretends at each stage

  • Tow: a rigid box stands in for the net, and a 7-hour tow lasts 0.3 seconds of simulated time.
  • Surface: gravity is turned 90° to stand in for the bag tipping.
  • Ramp: gravity is tilted to 35°, an unmeasured guess.
  • Lift: gravity back to vertical, dry. No swinging.
  • Deck: the 100 shrimp drop 1 m onto a hard floor, with nothing landing on top of them.

Why it can't be used

  • The first campaign failed the wall check: pretend shrimp poked through the box walls.
  • Halving the time step changed the deck result by 25.5%, against a 20% limit.
  • Doubling the stiffness of the spike tips moved the peak forces by about 40%, against a 10% limit.
  • Moving one shrimp's start by a ten-billionth of a metre changed the ramp result by 20%.
  • Codex's own verdict: "no physical convergence, measured crushing or survival result is established".

The models we set aside

Why only the column model is used, and what it would take to use the others

Model 2 · particle model

Worth repairing for one question

It was meant to give model 1 how load is shared between shrimp in a pile, and whether each shrimp's load stays the same over time. Those are exactly the two choices that move Norway most (+34 and −24 points).

What would make it usable: piles packed within the measured range, a passing rods test, a body stiffness with a basis in published measurement, deep piles measured away from the walls, results that hold under a halved time step and a second random seed, and a rebuilt hand-over to model 1. How long: the plan below aims at a first answer by 17 November; full runs at 2,000 prawns would take months of computer time.

Model 3 · five-stage test

Not worth rerunning yet

It has all of model 2's problems, and on top of that its results moved by 20 to 40% under tiny numerical changes.

What would make it usable: everything for model 2 first, then passing its own checks, realistic stage lengths, and the rest of the catch on top at the deck. Following the same shrimp through a haul is a good idea, but it adds nothing until the shrimp in it behave like shrimp.

Model 4 · 27-fishery runs

Only for fisheries Jan needs

The computer part is small: the 192-combination rerun of 7 fisheries finished in one evening. The hard part is real inputs for 20 more fisheries: catch per bag, gear, ramp or no ramp, deck handling.

Finding real inputs is weeks of research per handful of fisheries, and some may not exist (our estimate). A ranking of 7 fisheries by gear type may already be enough; that is a question for Jan.

The gaps

What none of the models include

  • Alive at the killing step, which is Jan's real question. Every model stops at the deck.
  • Any death except crushing: no air exposure, heat, lack of oxygen or rough handling.
  • Temperature. It doesn't change shell strength or survival in any model.
  • Different sizes in one catch. Within a dice roll, every shrimp in a fishery is the same size.
  • Fish, stones and weed mixed in with the shrimp.
  • A measured force on any shrimp inside any codend. No study has measured one.
  • A published crush test on a whole live shrimp of any of these species.
  • Any tuning to field data. Nothing was adjusted to match the field studies.

The best ways in, without harming animals: published shell-strength and cuticle measurements, footage of bags on the ramp and hoist, shells scored in catch already landed, and alive and dead counts at the killing step. The repaired particle model can then answer the load-sharing question on the computer.

Question 2

Can we make accurate animations?

It depends on what the animation is meant to show. Accurate pictures of the real process and of the column model's maths are possible now. An accurate animation of shrimp actually breaking in a pile is not possible yet.

Possible now

The real process on a boat

A drawn animation of tow, haul, emptying, sorting and cooking or freezing, in the right order and looking like the real boats, using the film frames as reference. A 20-second version exists.

Limits: no measured bag sizes, drop heights or timings, so those parts are drawn from guesses. It must be labelled as an illustration. Effort: days of Blender work (our estimate).

Possible now

What the column model calculates

An animated diagram: the bag as 50 slices, each filling with colour as its pressure rises from tow to ramp to lift to deck, with the crushed share counting up beside it. It matches the model exactly, because it draws the model's own numbers.

Limits: it shows the model, not reality, and cannot show single shrimp breaking. Effort: one to two days (our estimate).

Single shrimp bending, cracking and being pierced inside a packed bag. Only the particle model has single shrimp, and its piles are still too loose and its bodies too stiff. An animation of it would look convincing and be wrong.
